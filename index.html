A quick guide to openings

About this opening

What is a chess opening?

An opening is the first stretch of a chess game — roughly the first 10 to 15 moves — where both players race to set up their army before the real fighting begins. A good opening does three things: it claims the center, it develops your pieces into useful positions, and it keeps your king safe.

Openings have names (Italian Game, Queen's Gambit, London System) because strong players have been studying these exact sequences for centuries. Each one is a well-tested plan for the early phase of the game.

What “theory” means

When you hear a move is “in theory” or “on book,” it just means the move matches one of these well-studied sequences. Theory isn't a test you passed. It's a label for moves that have been analyzed and approved by decades of strong play.

You don't need to memorize theory to play well. If you follow basic principles — control the center, develop pieces, castle early, don't move the same piece twice — you'll stumble into theory on your own. That's the whole point. Theory encodes good principles. Good principles reproduce theory.

Why learn openings at all?

  • You stop losing in the first 10 moves. Most casual games are decided before the middlegame even starts. A sound opening puts you in the game.
  • You get to the middlegame with a plan. Good openings hand you a healthy position and a clear idea of what to do next.
  • You learn the principles by playing them. Every mainline move in a classical opening teaches a principle. Play them enough and the principles become instinct.

Why these five openings?

The five openings below cover the complete White curriculum — from your first opening to tournament-ready weapons. Learn them in order and you'll have a real answer to almost any game.

Beginner-friendly

  • Italian Game C50

    The classic 1.e4 e5 opening. Directly claims the center, develops pieces toward the king, and teaches every core principle in its purest form.

  • London System D02

    A setup-based opening: you play the same healthy piece arrangement against almost anything Black does. Low on memorization, high on understanding.

Classical & strategic

  • Queen's Gambit Declined D30

    The classic 1.d4 opening. A solid, strategic setup where both sides fight for the center with pawns — famously popular at world-championship level.

  • Ruy Lopez C84

    The Spanish Game — the deepest and most respected 1.e4 e5 opening. A natural next step after the Italian, and a masterclass in slow, positional pressure.

Flexible hypermodern

  • English Opening A29

    Control the center from the flank with 1.c4. A hypermodern weapon that transposes into dozens of systems — favored by Botvinnik, Kasparov, and Carlsen.

Reading the coach panel

After each of your moves, the coach scores it on two independent axes so you know both how principled the move was and how it compares to established theory.

  • Quality — Was the move sound? This is based on the engine's evaluation, regardless of whether it's a known theory move. A great move that nobody has ever played still scores high here.
  • Theory — Did your move match the main line of the opening you're studying? “In theory” means yes. “Off theory” just means you left the book — not that you played badly.

Below those, a four-pill rubric tracks the four things a good opening move should do: Center control, Development, King safety, and Tempo. Each pill lights up when your move earns it.

When do you “know” an opening?

You don't need to memorize 20 moves deep. You're ready to move on when the first 8–10 moves feel automatic and you can say out loud what each one is trying to do. That's when the principles have sunk in. Then pick the next opening and repeat.

What about playing Black?

For now, every opening here is studied from the White side — you're always the one making the first move. Black-side curricula (the Sicilian, the Caro-Kann, the Slav, and more) are coming soon.

Where does opening theory come from?

Every move in a known opening has been tested in thousands — sometimes millions — of games by strong human players and, more recently, by chess engines analyzing billions of positions per second. The mainline is the move that has, over time, proven to give the best results. When you play a mainline move, you're standing on centuries of accumulated analysis.

That's why the coach says “theory” rather than “correct.” Theory isn't the only good move — it's the most battle-tested one.
